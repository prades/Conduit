With SAVE NOTE on, tapping a key writes that pitch out as a WAV — looped and enveloped exactly as you hear it, so a 200 ms fragment becomes a full sustained note. Master FX are not baked in; the file is the dry instrument.
SEQUENCER
What this does. Console music is not stored as audio — it is sequence data driving a sound chip, plus a bank of instrument samples. Those samples are real audio. This scans a ROM for BRR blocks, the SNES sample format, decodes them exactly, and hands you the results as playable instruments.
Why the samples are so short. They are meant to be. A SNES instrument is typically 100–300 ms that the hardware loops while an envelope shapes the note — a cymbal ringing for two seconds is a fraction of a second of audio held open. Leave SUSTAIN on and use DECAY to set how long a note lasts; LOOP FROM skips past the attack so the held part is the steady portion rather than the initial transient. Switch to ONE SHOT for percussion.
Splitting audio. SPLIT AUDIO takes an MP3 or any audio file and separates it into drums, bass, vocal and instrumental stems. There is no neural network involved — it uses harmonic/percussive separation (median-filtering the spectrogram, so sustained content and transients come apart) plus centre extraction for the vocal. Drums and bass come out well; the vocal split is the weakest, since it relies on the lead being centre-panned. Not Spleeter, but it runs entirely on your device. SPLIT LEN caps how much is analysed — longer takes proportionally longer.
Where the ROM scan works. Best on SNES ROMs, since BRR is theirs. Other systems store samples in formats this does not decode, so expect few or no hits — the scanner only reports runs that are structurally valid, rather than guessing.
Your files stay here. Nothing is uploaded and no ROM is included; load one you dumped yourself. Audio you extract is someone's copyrighted work, same as any other sample — fine for your own use, worth clearing before you release anything built on it.
